Website

A short guide to briefing a website designer

The smoother a website project goes, the clearer the brief was at the start.

Most website projects that go off track don't fail because of design taste — they fail because the designer was working from a vague starting point and had to guess. A good brief removes the guesswork. It doesn't need to be long or technical; it just needs to answer the handful of questions that actually shape the work.

Start with what the site needs to do

Before anything about colours or layout, be clear on the job the site has to do. Is it there to generate leads, sell products directly, explain your services, or simply act as a credible home base people land on after finding you elsewhere? Naming the primary goal up front helps a designer prioritise the right pages and calls to action, instead of treating every section as equally important.

Say who it's for

A site aimed at busy local customers booking a service reads very differently to one aimed at enterprise buyers doing due diligence. Describe your typical visitor — what they're looking for, what might make them hesitate, and what would convince them to get in touch. This shapes tone, length of copy, and how much proof (reviews, case studies, credentials) needs to be visible early.

Show, don't just tell

Words like "clean," "modern," or "professional" mean different things to different people. Instead, gather two or three sites you genuinely like — even from unrelated industries — and say specifically what you like about each: the layout, the pace, the imagery, the way it handles navigation. It's just as useful to point out a site you dislike and why. Examples close the gap between what you're imagining and what a designer pictures far faster than adjectives do.

Give the practical details early

A brief should also cover the unglamorous logistics: how many pages you expect, whether you already have a logo and brand colours or need those created too, what content (text, photos) you can supply versus what needs to be written or sourced, and any must-have features — a booking system, a shop, multi-language support. Flagging these early avoids expensive scope surprises halfway through the project.

Set a realistic timeline and decision process

Finally, be upfront about your timeline and who needs to approve the work. A project with five stakeholders each reviewing separately moves very differently to one with a single decision-maker. Naming this at the start — along with any hard deadlines, like a launch event or campaign — helps the designer plan revisions and avoid last-minute bottlenecks.

None of this needs to be a formal document. A page of clear notes covering these five areas is usually enough to save both sides significant back-and-forth, and gets you to a site you're happy with faster.
